Detroit Has the Talent. How Do We Build More Sustainable Artist Careers?

What the 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Reveals About Building Sustainable Careers

The 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census documents an experienced, deeply connected creative community with extraordinary cultural influence. It also gives us new insight into what artists say they need to build sustainable careers. At Sentient Productions, I’m exploring whether proven software product development methods can help.

Detroit Doesn’t Need Anyone to Explain Its Musical Importance.

This city has repeatedly changed the way the world hears music.

Detroit techno didn’t emerge because someone identified a market opportunity. It grew from experimentation, collaboration, technology, imagination, and artists building something that didn’t exist before.

That spirit still lives here.

The 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census documents a creative community with tremendous depth. Eighty-eight percent of creative respondents have more than ten years of experience in music.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

This isn’t a city waiting to discover talent.

The talent is already here.

The more interesting question is:

How can we help more of that talent build sustainable careers around the work they’re already creating?

That’s a question I care deeply about, and it’s one Sentient Productions is beginning to explore.

The Artists Are Experienced. They’re Also Doing a Lot Themselves.

One of the findings that immediately caught my attention was just how much Detroit’s creatives manage on their own.

82% handle their own publicity and social media. 77% handle their own booking. 76% handle their own management.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

Think about everything contained inside those numbers.

An independent artist may also be their own:

  • Booking agent.
  • Manager.
  • Publicist.
  • Marketer.
  • Content creator.
  • Business developer.
  • Web producer.
  • Strategist.

And somewhere in there, they’re supposed to find the time and creative energy to actually make music.

One Census respondent described wanting access to booking and promotional support so artists could spend more time concentrating on what they do best.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

That resonated with me.

Not because artists shouldn’t understand the business around their careers. They absolutely should.

But being independent shouldn’t have to mean being an expert in every discipline required to sustain a career.

Detroit Artists Know How to Learn by Doing

Another finding feels particularly Detroit.

Sixty-nine percent of respondents said they developed their skills primarily through hands-on experience. Only 6% cited formal music-business education.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

That’s an enormous amount of knowledge built through practice.

Make something. Put it into the world. Learn. Make the next thing better.

There’s something strikingly familiar about that process to me.

Because it’s also how the best modern software products are developed.

And that’s where my two worlds intersect.

What Does Software Product Development Have to Do With Electronic Artists?

A lot, it turns out.

I’ve spent more than twenty-five years helping teams develop products and emerging technologies.

Modern product development moved away from the idea that you should disappear for years, build something enormous based on assumptions, launch it, and hope you guessed correctly.

Instead, good product teams work iteratively.

Research. Define. Prioritize. Build. Launch. Measure. Learn. Improve.

You don’t assume you understand the people you’re building for. You talk to them.

You don’t build everything you could build. You determine what you should build.

You test assumptions before making enormous investments.

You measure what happens after something launches.

And when the evidence tells you something isn’t working? You change it.

Sentient Productions takes those proven software product development strategies and practices and applies them to artist development.

Right now, I’m focused specifically on electronic artists.

Not because artists are products. They’re not.

But careers can be developed intentionally.

Start With the Artist, Not the Tactics

The music business can make it very easy to start with solutions.

  • “I need more followers.”
  • “I need a website.”
  • “I need an EPK.”
  • “I need a booking agent.”
  • “I need press.”
  • “I need merchandise.”
  • “I need to post more.”

Maybe.

Product development teaches us to ask another question first:

What problem are we trying to solve?

For an artist, that means starting further upstream.

  • Who are you?
  • What makes your work distinctly yours?
  • What are you trying to build?
  • Who connects with it?
  • What does success mean to you?
  • What’s standing between where you are and where you want to go?

Then we decide what to build.

That might be positioning. It might be a website. It might be an EPK. It might be a release strategy. It might be booking infrastructure. It might be partnerships. It might be licensing. It might be new revenue streams.

Sometimes the answer might be:

Don’t build anything yet. Learn more first.

That’s product thinking.

Detroit Artists Are Already Investing Heavily in Their Careers

Getting those decisions right matters because independent artists have finite resources.

Creative respondents to the Census spend an average of $8,965 annually on their music careers, including recording, equipment, rehearsal space, promotion and PR, merchandise and professional services.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

At the same time, average annual music income among respondents is approximately $20,000, with music accounting for an average of 22% of their total income.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

That’s real money.

And it makes prioritization incredibly important.

An artist shouldn’t spend thousands of dollars solving the wrong problem simply because someone told them that’s what musicians are supposed to do next.

One of the most useful ideas product development can bring to artist development is remarkably simple:

Before making a large investment, validate the assumption behind it.

Signal Before Scale

This is also the thinking behind one of Sentient’s core principles: Signal Before Scale.

The Census identifies audience development as an important need across Greater Detroit’s music ecosystem. Sixty-seven percent of venue and presenter respondents identify audience-development tools as a major need.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

When audiences aren’t growing, the natural reaction is often to increase promotion.

More content. More advertising. More posting. More reach.

But product development would ask: Are we ready to scale?

If an artist’s identity isn’t clear, more visibility doesn’t create clarity.

If people can’t quickly understand what makes the artist distinctive, reaching more people doesn’t solve that.

That’s why Sentient works on the signal first.

Clarify the artist. Clarify the story. Understand the audience. Establish the direction. Build the foundation. Then amplify it.

There Isn’t One Definition of Success

The Census also reinforces something I think artist development needs to respect.

Detroit artists don’t all want the same career.

Thirty-eight percent of respondents define success locally, including paid performances and active participation in the local scene. Thirty-five percent define it regionally. Twenty-seven percent are pursuing national or international careers.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

All three are legitimate.

Product teams establish what success means before deciding what to optimize.

Artists deserve the same consideration.

One artist may want to tour internationally. Another may want to become a respected regional performer. Another may want to build a label. Another may want to develop licensing revenue. Another may want to produce records and rarely perform. Another may want several of those things.

The objective isn’t to impose someone else’s definition of success.

It’s to ask: What career do you want to build?

Then we can build toward it intentionally.

Detroit Already Has an Extraordinary Ecosystem

One of my favorite conclusions from the Census is that Greater Detroit’s challenge isn’t necessarily a lack of resources, but how effectively those resources connect.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

That distinction matters.

Detroit already has extraordinary artists.

It has producers, DJs, musicians, labels, venues, promoters, studios, engineers, educators, designers, publicists, managers, booking professionals, technologists and generations of hard-earned knowledge.

It has world-class scenes in techno, hip-hop, jazz, gospel, rock and beyond. Census respondents themselves describe tremendous community and collaboration alongside a desire for greater connection between those networks.

Source: 2026 Greater Detroit Music Census Report →

Sentient doesn’t need to replace any of that.

I want to help artists navigate it.

A publicist can do extraordinary work, but the artist should be ready for publicity.

A booking professional can open doors, but the artist needs a clear live proposition.

A designer can create a beautiful identity, but they need to understand what that identity is communicating.

A developer can build a website, but someone needs to determine what job that website is supposed to do.

My role is often to help connect those pieces around a clear strategy.

That’s What We’re Testing With Artist Systems v1.0

This fall, Sentient Productions will begin an eight-month pilot with three Metro Detroit electronic artists.

I’m calling it Artist Systems v1.0 because I’m approaching the work exactly as I would the development of a new product.

We’ll establish where each artist is today. We’ll understand where they want to go.

We’ll research. We’ll identify problems. We’ll prioritize. We’ll build. We’ll launch. We’ll measure. We’ll learn. And we’ll iterate.

The three artists aren’t test subjects.

They’re Founding Partners.

We’ll build the framework together.

The artists will leave with career infrastructure built specifically around their goals. Sentient will leave with evidence about what worked, what didn’t, what needs to change, and which parts of the methodology can be repeated for other artists.

The process will become a documented case study, and the findings will be shared.

Because if we learn something useful, I don’t want that knowledge to stop with three artists.

Leave the Culture Stronger Than You Found It

That’s one of the principles behind Sentient Productions.

And it’s particularly important to me here.

Detroit created a musical language that traveled around the planet.

Electronic music continues to grow, evolve and generate enormous cultural and economic value around the world.

The artists carrying Detroit’s musical legacy forward deserve every opportunity to participate in that future.

I have a particular set of skills from another industry.

Product strategy. Research. Positioning. Experience design. Rapid experimentation. Roadmapping. Measurement. Iteration. Systems thinking.

I’m interested in what happens when those tools are placed in service of artists.

Not to change the culture. To contribute to it.

Not to tell Detroit how to make music. Detroit already taught the world that.

But perhaps some of what software product development has learned about turning ideas into sustainable products can help artists turn extraordinary creative work into more sustainable careers.

That’s the experiment.
