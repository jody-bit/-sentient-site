01

The assignment

At first glance, an Electronic Press Kit (EPK) seems simple. A bio. A couple of songs. Some photos. A video. A list of notable shows. Contact information.

Put it on a page, make it look good, send it to promoters.

But a useful EPK isn’t simply a collection of those things. It has to answer a much bigger question:

What does someone need to understand about this artist in order to take the next professional step with them?

Detroit Trouble is a Detroit power trio formed in 2015. The band came to Sentient Productions for a booking-focused EPK that could communicate who they are now: their music, live credibility, career history, media recognition, production information and the practical details someone needs to book them.

The finished piece is deliberately concise. It features two recordings, two official videos, selected direct-support credits, notable venues and events, a short band story, current photography, press recognition, production information, social channels and booking contact information.

That’s what the audience sees. Getting there required considerably more than arranging those ingredients on a page.

02

Before design comes discovery

I approached the EPK the way I would approach a product.

Before deciding what it should look like, I needed to understand what it needed to do, who would use it, what information mattered to those users and what Detroit Trouble wanted them to understand.

The primary audience was straightforward: talent buyers, promoters, venues, festivals and other music-industry contacts. Their needs shaped the information hierarchy.

  1. Can I hear the band?
  2. Can I see them perform?
  3. What kind of act are they?
  4. Do they have meaningful live experience?
  5. Who have they supported?
  6. Where have they played?
  7. What does the band look like now?
  8. What does production require?
  9. How do I book them?

Those questions became the architecture of the EPK.

Start with the user and the job the product needs to perform, then decide what belongs in it. That is product thinking applied to artist development.

03

The music had to come first

One of the most important parts of the project happened before the visual design began. I listened.

Genre labels are useful shorthand, but they aren’t necessarily enough to understand an artist. Detroit Trouble uses “blue collar blues rock,” and that language is part of how the band describes itself. Listening closely to the current material revealed something more specific: the blues is foundational, but the contemporary sound carries considerably more hard-rock weight, physicality and force.

That was a matter of listening closely enough to translate the music for the people who would be hearing it cold: Detroit hard rock with blues underneath it.

If I had started with the existing imagery and terminology alone, it would have been easy to build an EPK around familiar visual shorthand: Detroit, automobiles, mechanics, blue-collar culture, blues-rock iconography. Those things are authentically part of Detroit Trouble’s world. But they aren’t the product.

The band is the product. The music is the reason the rest matters.

The final EPK therefore moves almost immediately from the band introduction into LISTEN, followed by WATCH and LIVE PROOF. The supporting story and visual identity come after the audience has been given access to the work itself.

04

Ten years of a career don’t arrive in a neat folder

Independent artists accumulate careers organically.

A song is released here. A photographer shoots a show there. Someone opens for a national act. A local television station does a feature. A lineup evolves. Social platforms change. Videos live on YouTube. Technical information lives somewhere else. A great photograph might be on someone’s phone.

None of this is unusual. It is the natural byproduct of making music while also doing the thousand other things required to keep an independent band moving. An EPK asks us to take that history and compress it.

For Detroit Trouble, that meant reviewing current and historical material, listening to recordings, watching performance footage, identifying the current lineup, reviewing photography, researching media appearances, distinguishing the strongest career highlights, checking names and credits, gathering links and deciding which material best represented the band today.

Some assets arrived during the project. Others required research. Some information needed confirmation. Some older material remained useful as career history even when newer material better represented the current band.

That process isn’t an inconvenience surrounding the creative work. It is part of the creative work. And it takes time.

05 · Curation is labor
The work isn’t valuable because everything was included. The work is valuable because everything didn’t need to be.

Curation is labor

This project reinforced something that is easy to underestimate. A one-page deliverable can contain many hours of invisible work.

The final EPK does not show every photograph considered, every video watched, every recording reviewed, every career credit researched or every creative direction explored. It shouldn’t.

The point of professional curation is that the audience doesn’t have to experience all of that. They get the result of the decisions.

For Detroit Trouble, years of activity were distilled into a deliberately selective LIVE PROOF section: formation in Detroit in 2015, national touring availability, selected direct-support appearances and a short group of meaningful venues and events.

06

Turning lived experience into positioning

The interviews and music review also uncovered the connective tissue between the band’s sound and its identity.

Detroit Trouble’s working-class character isn’t simply a visual theme. The members’ experiences with work, poverty, anxiety, addiction and persistence inform the material itself. That became the basis of the finished artist story:

Detroit Trouble turns working-class Detroit pressure into blues-rooted hard rock built for release.

The finished bio connects those experiences to heavy guitar, physical rhythm, blues vocabulary, musicianship and live showmanship rather than treating “blue collar” as a costume or marketing category.

07

Art direction without redesigning the artist

Sentient’s job wasn’t to invent a new Detroit Trouble. It was to make the existing artist easier to understand.

The band’s automotive imagery, working-class references and established logo were retained, but given a supporting role. The musicians and the music became primary.

The visual system uses a restrained industrial palette, heavy typography, documentary photography and generous structural divisions. The opening image presents the three musicians without instruments beside the oversized Detroit Trouble logo. There’s a little mystery in it. Then the music starts doing the explaining.

As the page progresses, photography becomes more active. The final press-photo section incorporates live performance imagery, bringing movement back into the presentation after the quieter band portrait and informational sections.

The result is unmistakably Detroit Trouble without becoming a collage of every possible Detroit-rock visual cue.

08

From static asset to career infrastructure

The project also changed the way I think about EPKs. An EPK isn’t merely promotional collateral.

To make one genuinely useful, an artist needs many of the basic components of a functioning professional career: representative music, a clear identity, current photography, performance footage, career history, credible professional accomplishments, press, technical information, working links, booking contact information and an organized place for professional assets.

Detroit Trouble’s finished EPK connects those pieces. A promoter can hear the band, watch them, evaluate their experience, understand their story, access press photography, find production information, reach their platforms and contact them about a booking without having to reconstruct the band from a trail of social posts.

That makes the EPK more than a sales sheet. It becomes a professional front door to the artist’s working career.

09

What a “simple” deliverable contains

A finished EPK can look deceptively straightforward.

The visible production work includes writing, art direction, layout and final delivery. But underneath it is another layer of professional work: interviews, listening, research, media review, asset gathering, fact-checking, selection, organization, client collaboration and revision.

Most of that work disappears into the finished piece. That’s intentional.

The person receiving an EPK shouldn’t have to sort through years of photographs, recordings, videos, press, performances and career history. The value of the finished tool is that those decisions have already been made.

This project also reinforced an important distinction in how Sentient scopes artist work.

EPK production

When an artist arrives with current, organized and approved materials, producing an EPK is largely an exercise in positioning, curation, writing, art direction and design.

Additional artist-development work

When the project also requires identifying representative material, reviewing archives, researching career history, developing missing pieces or organizing professional assets, that work belongs to the broader practice of artist development.

Neither situation is unusual. They simply require different amounts and kinds of work.

The page may be simple because the process was thorough.

10

What Detroit Trouble taught Sentient

This project began as an EPK. It ended up validating a much broader idea behind Sentient Productions.

Independent artists don’t only need individual deliverables. They need the connective thinking that helps their music, identity, assets, history, opportunities and professional materials work together.

Sometimes that work produces an EPK. Sometimes it produces an artist-development plan, a release strategy, a booking package, a visual system or a clearer understanding of what needs to happen next.

My product-development background turns out to be particularly useful here because independent music careers behave a lot like products in development. There are users. There are goals. There are requirements. There are dependencies. There are missing inputs. There are assumptions to test. There is accumulated history. There are competing priorities. And eventually, somebody has to decide what matters enough to ship.

The art direction matters. The writing matters. The photography matters. But before any of those things can work, someone has to understand what we’re building, who it’s for, and why it deserves to exist.

That’s the work I mean when I call myself an Independent Artist Architect. Detroit Trouble is a hard-rock band, and the method worked the same way it would for any other artist: it belongs to Sentient, and the creative expression belongs to the artist.

And sometimes, the finished result is only one page.
